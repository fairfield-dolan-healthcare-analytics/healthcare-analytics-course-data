# Mini-seminar

Once during the term, you run a **15-minute mini-seminar** for the class on a topic you propose and the instructor approves. You bring a **view**: something you think is true about healthcare data and are willing to defend. Then you lead the discussion. The goal is to get everyone, you included, readier for the project decisions ahead.

## When

| Week | Theme your topic connects to | Slots |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Verifying sourcing: where numbers come from, and how they go wrong | 2 |
| 5 | Defending a number: rates, denominators, suppression, risk adjustment | 2 |
| 6 | Validating the use: is this the right data for this decision? | 2 |
| 7 | Compliance: PHI, de-identification, what may go into an AI tool | 2 |

You pick your week in week 1. First come, first served.

## Your seminar replaces one deliverable

In your seminar week, **the seminar is your graded deliverable** (it counts as one of the 12 weekly deliverables). You don't submit that week's regular deliverable. If a later week builds on it (your three traces in week 4, the rate you defend in week 5, your data card in week 6, or the compliance checklist in week 7), hand in that piece with the following week's work. It's checked for completion only, not graded.

## Step 1: Propose a topic (due before the week 3 class)

Send the instructor this paragraph. It takes about 15 minutes. **Presenting in week 4?** Send yours before the week 2 class instead, so you have time to prepare. You'll hear back by the weekend, either approved or with a suggestion.

- **Week:**
- **Topic** (one line):
- **My view** (one sentence you'd defend, not a summary; for example, "Hospital star ratings punish hospitals that serve poorer patients", rather than "An overview of star ratings"):
- **One or two sources** I'll read myself (a paper, a CMS or CDC methods page, a news investigation):
- **Why it matters for our projects** (one sentence):

Good topics are ones a classmate's aggregator or app might run into. Some starting points (propose your own if you can):

- How CMS builds hospital star ratings, and why hospitals dispute them (week 4)
- Risk adjustment: does it make a comparison between hospitals fair? (week 5)
- Small numbers and suppression: what we lose when a cell is hidden (week 5)
- Model-based estimates like PLACES: what a county "estimate" is and isn't (week 6)
- The ecological fallacy in health data (week 6)
- Re-identification: how "de-identified" data has been traced back to people (week 7)
- HIPAA Safe Harbor vs. Expert Determination (week 7)
- An algorithm that under-served patients it was meant to help (Obermeyer et al., 2019) (week 9)
- A widely used sepsis prediction model, tested outside the hospitals that built it (week 9)
- Where AI chat tools fail on health questions, and how anyone would know (week 10)

## Step 2: Prepare (about 2–3 hours)

1. Read your sources yourself. An AI tool can help you find and summarize them, but you have to have read what you cite.
2. Write a **one-page handout**: your view, the two or three points that support it, the strongest point against it, and your sources. Post it in the class channel by the Sunday before your seminar.
3. Prepare **two discussion questions** that classmates can answer from their own projects. "What does this mean for your aggregator?" is better than "What do you think?"
4. **Defend every number.** Any number on your handout or screen follows the course standard: where it came from, what it counts, what was left out, and what period it covers.

## Step 3: Run it (15 minutes)

- **About 7 minutes:** your view and why. Slides are optional; one screen or the handout is enough. No AI tool open while you present.
- **About 8 minutes:** you lead the discussion with your two questions. You call on people, keep it moving, and close with one sentence on what you heard.

## How it's graded

| | Strong | Needs work |
|---|---|---|
| **A view** | A clear claim you defend and can be argued with. | A summary of a topic with no position. |
| **Sources** | You've read them and can answer questions about them; numbers meet the course standard. | Claims you can't trace, or a source you only know from an AI summary. |
| **Against** | You state the strongest opposing point fairly. | Only one side. |
| **Tied to the projects** | Classmates leave knowing what to check in their own work. | Interesting, but no link to what we're building. |
| **Discussion** | Questions people can answer from their own work; you lead, and you close it. | Questions with no traction, or the talk runs over the discussion. |
